See the dance
The Pursuermoves towards connection

What it looks like

  • Reaches out more when feeling disconnected
  • Wants to talk things through straight away
  • Asks questions, seeks reassurance
  • May follow their partner from room to room
  • Can become critical when needs are not met

What is underneath

  • A deep need to feel important and valued
  • Fear that silence means something is wrong
  • Reaching out is their way of trying to repair
Core fear: “If I stop reaching, we will drift apart.”
See the dance
The Avoidermoves away to self-protect

What it looks like

  • Needs space when things get intense
  • Goes quiet, shuts down, or leaves the room
  • Prefers to let things settle before talking
  • May seem calm outside but feels flooded inside
  • Can appear dismissive or uninterested

What is underneath

  • A deep need to feel competent and not overwhelmed
  • Fear that engaging will make things worse
  • Withdrawing is their way of trying to keep the peace
Core fear: “If I engage, I will get it wrong or make it worse.”
Imago · Hendrix & Hunt

Two primal ways of staying safe

The maximiser

The Hailstorm

Maximises the conflict. Expresses feelings loudly and passionately, needing to be heard.

For a hailstorm, silence feels like abandonment.
The minimiser

The Turtle

Minimises the conflict. Retreats into the shell, holds feelings in, avoids confrontation.

For a turtle, an intense conversation feels like an attack.

The louder the hailstorm hails, the deeper the turtle retreats. Not personality flaws, adaptations learned in childhood.

The trap
reaches, pushes, asks again
feels like a storm coming
goes quiet and pulls away
feels like abandonment
round and round it goes

Each partner's way of staying safe is exactly what sets off the other.

Why we call it the dance
These patterns are dances. The dance cannot continue in the same way if one partner changes their steps.
After Harriet Lerner, The Dance of Anger (1985), the classic work on pursuer and distancer cycles.

That is the hope in this. Nobody has to wait for their partner to go first.

The part to hold onto
It is not your characters.
It is a cycle.

Not a nag. Not cold. Those are the roles the dance hands out. The dance itself is the problem, and a cycle can be changed.

Stepping out · 1 of 3
1

Name the dance,
not the partner

The moment either partner spots the pattern, say so gently. It turns the couple against the cycle, instead of against each other.

“I think we are in our dance right now. Can we slow down?”
Stepping out · 2 of 3
2

The turtle says when

Space is allowed. A pause with a return time feels safe. A pause without one feels like abandonment.

“I need half an hour. I am not going anywhere, and I will come back at eight.”
Stepping out · 3 of 3
3

The storm softens

Letting the space happen, trusting the return. Easing off is not giving up on the conversation. It is what makes the conversation possible.

“Okay. Eight works. Thank you for telling me.”
Your one action this week
Just notice the dance, and notice which step is yours.

That is the whole homework. The storms get shorter, the shell gets shallower, and noticing is the first step out.
