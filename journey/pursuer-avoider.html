Understanding Your Pattern

The Dance: Pursuer and Avoider

Why the harder one of you tries to talk, the faster the other one disappears. This page explains the most common pattern couples bring to coaching, and how to step out of it together.

Does this sound familiar?

Something small sets it off. One of you wants to sort it out right now, so you follow your partner into the kitchen, still explaining your point. The other goes quiet, finds something that suddenly needs doing, and leaves the room.

An hour later nothing is resolved. One of you feels shut out. The other feels hounded. And the silence in the house is somehow louder than the argument was.

What is actually happening

When a couple feels disconnected, each partner protects themselves, and most of us protect ourselves in one of two opposite directions. One moves towards. One moves away. Neither move is wrong, and neither is chosen on purpose. They are instincts, usually learned long before you met each other.

The Pursuer

moves towards connection

The pursuer cannot relax while something is unresolved. They want to talk about it now, fix it now, feel close again now. They ask questions, seek reassurance, sometimes follow from room to room. Reaching out is their way of trying to repair, even when it comes out sharp.

The Avoider

moves away to self-protect

The avoider feels flooded when things get intense. They go quiet, need space, and prefer to let things settle before talking. They can look calm or even uninterested on the outside while feeling overwhelmed on the inside. Withdrawing is their way of keeping the peace and not making things worse.

Why it becomes a cycle

Here is the trap. Each partner's way of staying safe is exactly the thing that sets off the other. The pattern feeds itself, which is why it never feels finished and why it starts faster each time.

The loop, one trigger at a time
The pursuer reaches, pushes, asks again
…which feels like a storm coming, so…
…which feels like abandonment, so…
the avoider goes quiet and pulls away
↻
Round and round it goes. The more one pursues, the more the other withdraws. The more one withdraws, the harder the other pursues.

What is underneath

Under the surface, both of you are afraid of the same thing, losing each other. You just show it in opposite ways.

The pursuer's fear
“If I stop reaching, we will drift apart.” Silence feels dangerous, so they fill it.
The avoider's fear
“If I engage, I will get it wrong and make it worse.” Conflict feels dangerous, so they leave it.

Both fears make sense. Neither of you is the villain in this story. You are two people using opposite strategies to protect the same relationship.

Same dance, different names

Relationship researchers have been describing this pattern for decades. Harville Hendrix, who created Imago therapy, calls the two roles the hailstorm and the turtle. When things get tense, the hailstorm gets louder and bigger, and the turtle pulls deeper into its shell. The harder it hails, the deeper the turtle hides.

Sue Johnson, who developed Emotionally Focused Therapy, calls the same pattern pursue and withdraw, and found it sits underneath most repeating arguments. Different researchers, same dance. If you remember one image, remember the hailstorm and the turtle.

The part to hold onto

It is not your characters. It is a cycle. And a cycle can be changed.

You are not a nag, and your partner is not cold. Those are the roles the dance hands you. The dance itself is the problem, and naming it is how you both step off the floor.

How couples step out

1

Name the dance, not the partner

The moment either of you spots the pattern, say so gently. Naming it turns the two of you against the cycle, instead of against each other.

“I think we are in our dance right now. Can we slow down?”
2

The turtle says when

Space is allowed. What changes everything is the turtle coming out of the shell just far enough to say when they will be back. A pause with a return time feels safe. A pause without one feels like abandonment.

“I need half an hour. I am not going anywhere, and I will come back at eight.”
3

The storm softens

The pursuer's job is to let that space happen, trusting the return. Easing off is not giving up on the conversation. It is what makes the conversation possible.

“Okay. Eight works. Thank you for telling me.”

What changes with practice

The dance does not disappear, and it does not need to. What changes is that you both start to see it sooner, name it faster, and recover from it quicker. The storms get shorter. The shell gets shallower. And over time, the energy you spent on the cycle becomes energy you spend on each other. Start by simply noticing the dance this week, and noticing which step is yours. Noticing is the first step out.
