The Dance · The Science

Why couples get stuck in the same argument

The pursuer and avoider pattern is the most studied conflict dynamic in relationship research. This is the deeper read behind the video, for anyone who likes to understand the why before the how.

Written for the readers, take your time

The pattern, in one paragraph

When a couple feels disconnected, each partner protects themselves, and most of us protect ourselves in one of two opposite directions. One partner moves towards: they reach out, want to talk it through, seek reassurance, and feel anxious while anything sits unresolved. The other partner moves away: they go quiet, need space, and feel flooded when things get intense. The trouble is that each partner's way of feeling safe is exactly the thing that sets off the other. The more one reaches, the more the other withdraws, and the more one withdraws, the harder the other reaches. It becomes a loop that feeds itself.

The pursuer (moves towards)

Reaches out, wants to resolve it now, seeks reassurance. Underneath: a fear that if they stop reaching, the two of you will drift apart.

The avoider (moves away)

Goes quiet, needs space, lets things settle. Underneath: a fear that engaging will only make things worse.

Four schools of research, one dance

What is striking about this pattern is that researchers from very different traditions kept arriving at the same place, and each gave it a name. They are describing the same dance from four angles.

Harriet Lerner
The Dance of Anger (1985)

Lerner, a clinical psychologist in the family systems tradition, was the first to popularise the idea that couples fall into recurring "dances", including the pursuer and distancer cycle. Her central insight is the one we lean on most: the pattern is circular, both people take part, and it cannot continue in the same way if one person changes their steps. She also reframed anger not as something to suppress, but as information, a signal pointing to where something needs to change. The hopeful part is that you never have to wait for your partner to move first.

Harville Hendrix & Helen LaKelly Hunt
Imago Relationship Therapy

Hendrix and Hunt gave the two roles their most memorable images. Under stress, the maximiser turns the energy up and out, expressing loudly and passionately, needing to be heard. They called this the hailstorm. The minimiser turns the energy down and in, containing it and retreating to protect themselves. They called this the turtle. Their observation, after decades of clinical work, is that turtles and hailstorms almost always end up together: the very difference that first attracts becomes the difference that escalates conflict. Crucially, they framed these not as personality flaws but as adaptations learned in childhood.

Dr Sue Johnson
Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)

Johnson grounded the same pattern in attachment science. In EFT it is called pursue and withdraw, and it is understood as the visible surface of a deeper attachment need. The pursuit is a protest against disconnection, a way of saying "are you there for me?", and the withdrawal is a way of managing overwhelm and not getting it wrong. The work of EFT is to help each partner see the soft feeling underneath the hard behaviour, so the couple can turn towards each other rather than against the cycle.

Professor Andrew Christensen
Couples research, UCLA

Christensen and colleagues studied the pattern empirically over decades and called it demand and withdraw. Their research found it is one of the most corrosive dynamics in distressed relationships, linked to lower satisfaction and higher rates of separation. They also found the roles are not fixed by gender: while one common version is one partner demanding change and the other withdrawing, who takes which role shifts depending on whose issue is on the table. When each partner raises their own concern, the roles can reverse entirely.

What the evidence actually shows

This is not just a clinical observation. In 2014, Schrodt, Witt and Shimkowski published a meta-analysis, a study that pools the results of many separate studies, covering 74 of them. They found that the demand and withdraw pattern was associated with lower relationship satisfaction more strongly than any other communication pattern they examined.

Across 74 studies, no communication pattern predicted relationship unhappiness more strongly than this one.
Schrodt, Witt & Shimkowski (2014), Communication Monographs

In other words, of all the things couples do when they argue, this particular dance is the one most consistently tied to distress. That is worth knowing, not to alarm you, but because it explains why naming and interrupting it matters so much, and why it is the first pattern most couples work on.

Why it becomes self-reinforcing

The reason the dance is so sticky is that it is built from two strategies that each make sense on their own, but that collide when put together.

To the pursuer, the partner's silence does not read as "I need a moment to settle". It reads as abandonment, as the relationship slipping away. So they do the thing that has always felt like repair: they reach harder. To the avoider, the partner's reaching does not read as "I love you and I want to feel close". It reads as a storm bearing down, as pressure they cannot meet without making it worse. So they do the thing that has always felt like safety: they retreat further.

Each move is a misread of the other's intention, and each misread intensifies the original defence. Neither partner is being unreasonable. They are two people using opposite tools to protect the same relationship, and the tools keep cancelling each other out.

Where the roles come from

Hendrix and Hunt's point about childhood matters here, because it changes blame into understanding. These styles are usually laid down long before you met your partner.

The hailstorm's history

Often grew up in a home where they were overlooked, or had to be loud to get their needs noticed. The lesson learned: if I express it strongly enough, I will finally be heard.

The turtle's history

Often grew up in a home that felt too intrusive or overwhelming. The lesson learned: if I go quiet and retreat far enough, I will finally be left in peace.

Seen this way, the behaviour that frustrates you most in your partner is usually a child's best attempt at staying safe, still running in an adult body. This is the turn that EFT and Imago both rely on.

When you see the scared child behind your partner's reaction, the anger begins to turn into empathy.
Efrat Fridman, Imago therapist

Stretching: the way out

Imago has a specific name for the work of changing the dance. It is called stretching, and it means deliberately doing the thing your instinct argues against. The turtle stretches towards staying and signalling; the hailstorm stretches towards softening and waiting. Neither comes naturally, and that is the point. The growth lives in the stretch.

In practice it comes down to three moves.

1

Name the dance, not the partner. The moment either of you spots the pattern, say so gently. Naming it turns the two of you against the cycle, instead of against each other. "I think we're in our dance right now. Can we slow down?"

2

The turtle says when. Space is allowed, and often necessary. What changes everything is the turtle coming out of the shell just far enough to give a return time. A pause with a return time feels safe. A pause without one feels like abandonment. "I need half an hour. I'm not going anywhere, and I'll come back at eight."

3

The storm softens. The pursuer's stretch is to let that space happen and trust the return. Easing off is not giving up on the conversation. It is the thing that makes the conversation possible. "Okay. Eight works. Thank you for telling me."

What changes with practice

The dance does not disappear, and it does not need to. Two people with different nervous systems will always have a pull towards their old protective styles. What changes is the speed of recovery. You both start to see the pattern sooner, name it faster, and climb out of it more quickly. The storms get shorter. The shell gets shallower. Over time, the energy that used to be spent going round the loop becomes energy you spend turning towards each other.

The goal was never to win the argument. It is to find your way back to each other, truly connected and fully present.
